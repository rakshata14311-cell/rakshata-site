Many of my ex-colleagues and I were responsible for creating an event that operated on the vague advice given by Deepak Chopra.

So this was February 2025; we were clocking in 11+ hours, working over the weekend, barely registering the exhaustion and burnout. It was all for a noble cause — at least that's what we told ourselves as we wrote speeches, created a guest itinerary, collaborated with external agencies, figured out the logistics and location, and manually ensured we were customising gift hampers and lanyards for 150+ audience members... the list goes on.

All of this labour was dedicated to making sure that this event — a wellness event held on the largest scale we could imagine — ended up being successful. It did, to a certain extent.

But something was missing. We always hear people talking about intent — how it's the intention that matters, not the outcome, right? Well, the intent here seemed to be concentrated wholly on "getting the sponsors," "finalising the venue," "inviting the most popular faces as speakers" (even if some of them had nothing substantial to speak about).

Understandably, there is a system to be followed if we are aiming for worthwhile traction. But when you are operating in the wellness industry, the priority ideally should be about the intent — i.e.:

- To help change the status quo's opinion about mental health
- To teach the new generation of change-makers to create policies that prioritise employee wellbeing
- To amplify the voices of leaders who dared to be vulnerable

You get my gist, right? The list goes on.

However, that wasn't the case here at all. I admit it — money plays a very important role. Enough capital needs to be secured to see the event through to its inevitable success. So chasing sponsors is obviously important, but my point is that it shouldn't be the end goal.

The concept of mental health in India is not really taken seriously. However, that changed enormously post-COVID. Institutions and companies suddenly poured significant capital into Employee Assistance Programs, primarily focused on mental health counselling. The pandemic that drove this welcome change became a central talking point at many mental health conferences; it shaped policies, engagement programs, and so on — and rightly so.

But it's 2026 now, and we haven't yet moved on.

When this concept was pitched to me, I was all in. Great, I thought — we get to be responsible for making real change. But alas, that's not to be.

There was a lot of learning, though — no way am I denying that. But the whole idea of "India's biggest wellness conference" was floated misleadingly. At the end of the day, it was an opportunistic way to trap people into becoming clients. It was an expensive, elaborate sales pitch.

The point I'm trying to get at is: India needs more of these wellness conferences. The Indian diaspora in the 21st century has never been more depressed, mentally disengaged, possibly suicidal. By organising empowering conferences that appeal to the change-makers of large organisations, we can help bring about the changes we need to ensure both Boomers and Gen Z employees prioritise their mental wellbeing in the face of modern chaos.

But —

The real, meaningful change these organisations aspire to bring is often lost in the midst of trying to get sponsors, guests, maintain the logistics, and make the event grand.

If you are a founder or CEO of a mental health organisation, your first and foremost responsibility must be to put yourself out there — be that cheesy LinkedIn leader, give TEDx talks, voice your opinions online and offline, create your own brand — in such a way that when you do plan to organise a wellness conference in the future, sponsors come knocking on your door, not the other way around. This will save enough time for you and your team. You can actually focus on the real thing: solving problems and bridging gaps.

I'm about to run out of my two cents, but the point is — we don't need more of these so-called wellness conferences that are organised to appease a set of people who have nothing to do with wellness.

My first-hand experience has been nothing short of disappointing, but I want yours to be different, better and welcoming. So if you are ever given a chance to speak up at one of these events — do it. Remember:

> Words are where most change begins. — Brandon Sanderson
